Short answer: yes, accountants can use ChatGPT, but how you use it matters a lot. The IRS Office of Professional Responsibility's June 24, 2026 guidance (Issue Number 2026-19) applies Circular 230 to AI. Its rule on client data is short: "Practitioners must strictly handle all client data using only secure, enterprise-approved AI." It also says you cannot rely on AI alone. A personal ChatGPT account fails that test. A firm-managed ChatGPT Business workspace, where OpenAI says it does not train on your data by default, is the kind of tool the guidance points you toward. It still does not settle the Section 7216 consent question for you.

That question is getting attention now because the 2027 filing season is less than three months away. Rightworks published a detailed Section 7216 explainer on November 3, 2026, and the IRS refreshed its "Protect your clients; protect yourself" page for tax pros on October 27. Below: what the IRS actually said, where the 7216 line sits, a checklist for a two- to five-person firm, and what ChatGPT Business costs a practice that size.

What the IRS Actually Said About AI in 2026

Issue Number 2026-19, "Introductory Guidelines for Responsible AI Use in Federal Tax Practice," does not create new rules. It maps existing Circular 230 sections onto AI use. The parts that matter most for a small firm:

  • Diligence (§ 10.22): "Practitioners cannot rely solely on AI; human scrutiny and editing are essential to ensure correctness and compliance with IRS expectations."
  • Fees (§ 10.27): billing for "manual labor or time that was not actually spent or double billing for AI-assisted tasks may violate § 10.27, depending on the facts."
  • Competence (§ 10.35): "Lack of technological competence could lead to improper advice or flawed filings."
  • Firm procedures (§ 10.36): "Firms must deploy internal policies and procedures for compliance with Circular 230 in the AI space."
  • Written advice (§ 10.37): "Blind reliance on what AI yields, especially when the underlying logic or sources are unclear, may constitute unreasonable reliance."
  • Taxpayer information (§ 10.51(a)(15), IRC 6713 and 7216): the document cites the civil and criminal preparer penalties, tells practitioners to "Never upload sensitive data to unsecured sites," and warns that "Willful mishandling of taxpayer information through AI may lead to disciplinary actions under Circular 230."

Note what the document leaves out. It never uses the word "consent." Some commentary goes further than the IRS did. Thomson Reuters' July 8 explainer says that where 7216-protected information is involved, "specific signed client consent is generally required before sharing data with any AI tool." That is Thomson Reuters' reading, not language from the OPR document. Rightworks takes the more cautious view that the IRS guidance "didn't settle the consent question."

The practical line: the IRS wants client data in "secure, enterprise-approved AI" only, a human checking every output, a written firm policy, and honest billing. A firm ChatGPT Business workspace covers the first item on the vendor side. The other three are firm decisions, and no software subscription makes them for you.

Section 7216: Where the Line Actually Sits

Section 7216 makes it a crime for a return preparer to knowingly or recklessly disclose or use tax return information without authorization. The regulations allow some disclosures without consent, and they require consent for others. The hard part with AI, as Rightworks puts it, is that "No exception in the regulations names generative AI tools." Its analysis, from a security lead at a firm that hosts accounting software:

  • "Entering client tax data into a consumer AI tool that can retain or reuse it is likely an unauthorized disclosure."
  • "A general confidentiality clause isn't a valid consent." Rightworks says 7216 consents must be separate documents for disclosure and for use, and that Form 1040 consents must follow Revenue Procedure 2013-14.
  • Any disclosure to a preparer, employee or contractor outside the US needs the client's signed consent first.

Until the IRS or your counsel says otherwise, here is a defensible way for a small firm to operate:

  1. Most AI work needs no tax return information at all. Engagement letters, a plain-English explanation of a CP2000 notice type, an Excel formula, a staff training outline, a newsletter on the new standard-deduction figures: none of these need a client's name, SSN or numbers.
  2. When a task needs client facts, strip identifiers first. "Married couple, two W-2s, a Schedule C with $40k net, moved states mid-year" is enough to draft a planning memo outline. Names, SSNs, EINs, addresses and account numbers stay out.
  3. If you want to put identifiable return information into an AI tool, get counsel's view and a 7216-compliant consent first. Use a firm-controlled business workspace only, never a personal account.

A Tax-Season AI Checklist for a 2–5 Person Firm

This is what we walk accounting firms through during onboarding. Most of it fits into an existing WISP review, which the IRS says FTC regulations already require you to have. The IRS points tax pros to Publication 4557, Safeguarding Taxpayer Data, and Publication 5709 on writing a Written Information Security Plan.

  1. Close the personal-account gap before January. The real risk is a preparer at 10 p.m. in March pasting a client's K-1 into a personal Plus account. Put everyone in one firm workspace and say in writing that client work goes nowhere else.
  2. Add AI to your WISP. Name the approved tool, who has access, what data may go in, and who reviews output. Keep a dated PDF of OpenAI's business data page in the file. That is your record of due diligence on "enterprise-approved AI."
  3. Write a one-page AI policy. OPR § 10.36 says firms "must deploy internal policies and procedures." One page is enough: approved uses, banned inputs (SSNs, EINs, bank details, full returns), the review rule, and who answers questions.
  4. Make "no identifiers" the default. Train staff to describe the fact pattern, not the client. It costs nothing on most tasks.
  5. Settle 7216 consent with counsel. If you ever want identifiable return information in an AI tool, use a separate, compliant consent, not a line in the engagement letter.
  6. Verify every authority. ChatGPT drafts and explains well. It is not a tax research database. Check every code section, regulation and ruling it mentions against the primary source before it goes in a memo or a return position.
  7. Bill for real time. OPR § 10.27 flags billing for time not spent. If AI cuts a two-hour memo to forty minutes, bill forty minutes, or move to fixed fees.
  8. Turn off seasonal staff on day one of their exit. Remove the seat in the admin console when you close their software access.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can accountants use ChatGPT with client information? ▼

Carefully. The IRS Office of Professional Responsibility's June 2026 guidance (Issue Number 2026-19) says practitioners must handle client data "using only secure, enterprise-approved AI" and cannot rely on AI without human review. That rules out personal accounts. Even in a business workspace, keep identifying tax return information out unless counsel has reviewed your Section 7216 position and you have a compliant consent.

Does Section 7216 apply to putting client data into ChatGPT? ▼

It can. Section 7216 restricts how preparers disclose and use tax return information, and no exception in the regulations names generative AI tools. Rightworks' November 2026 analysis says entering client tax data into a consumer AI tool that can retain or reuse it is likely an unauthorized disclosure, and the IRS's 2026 guidance did not settle when AI use needs client consent. Ask your counsel before entering identifiable return information into any AI tool.
