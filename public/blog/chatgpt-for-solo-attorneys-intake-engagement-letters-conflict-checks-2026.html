The trick is to use it on the process, not the client file. Draft your intake questionnaire, your engagement letter templates and your conflict-check workflow in ChatGPT. Run the actual client names, adverse parties and facts through the systems you already use for that. The bar opinions written so far line up behind that split. Solos ask us this more than any other group of lawyers, because they have no IT department and no general counsel's office to lean on. You are the managing partner, the ethics officer and the person who answers the intake line. What the Bar Opinions Say About Intake, Engagement Letters and Conflicts

Most state guidance applies existing rules rather than creating new ones. Three sources, all read on November 8, 2026, speak most directly to a solo's daily work:

  • ABA Formal Opinion 512 (July 2024). The ABA's announcement says a lawyer using generative AI must keep client information confidential "unless the client gives informed consent," that other rules "extend similar protections to former and prospective clients' information," and that "in most circumstances, the lawyer cannot charge a client for learning how to work a GAI tool."
  • Florida Bar Ethics Opinion 24-1 (January 19, 2024). The opinion says that if using a generative AI program "does not involve the disclosure of confidential information to a third-party, a lawyer is not required to obtain a client's informed consent." Where it does, the opinion says "it is recommended" that the lawyer get informed consent first. It also covers website intake chatbots and fees, discussed below.
  • New York City Bar Formal Opinion 2024-5 (August 2024). The opinion says a lawyer must not put confidential information into any generative AI system "that lacks adequate confidentiality and security protections" unless the client has given informed consent, and that a lawyer should "avoid entering details that can be used to identify the client." On conflicts, it says a firm "must ensure that the system implements any ethical screens required under the Rules."

California's 2026 guidance goes further on consent. We covered it, along with Alabama's Formal Opinion 2026-01, in Is ChatGPT Confidential for Lawyers? If you practice in California, read that post first.

The practical line for a solo: the opinions above care about one thing more than any other, which is whether confidential client information goes into the tool. Templates, checklists, questionnaires and workflows contain none. Build those in ChatGPT Business and keep client facts in your practice-management and conflict systems, and most of the consent question never comes up.

Client Intake: Use ChatGPT to Build the Form, Not to Run the Interview

Intake is where solos lose the most unbilled time, and it is also where the opinions are most specific. Florida's opinion notes that "law firms increasingly utilize website chatbots for client intake," and warns that a prospective-client relationship, "or even a lawyer-client relationship," can be created without the lawyer's knowledge. It says a lawyer "must inform prospective clients that they are communicating with an AI program," and that chatbots "must include a disclaimer indicating that the chatbot is an AI program and not a lawyer or employee of the law firm." The NYC opinion adds that a lawyer using a generative AI chatbot for intake "must adequately supervise the chatbot."

Putting ChatGPT itself on your website is not what ChatGPT Business is for, and for most solos the supervision burden is not worth it. Use it behind the scenes instead:

  • Intake questionnaires by practice area. Ask for a first-call questionnaire for an uncontested divorce, a landlord-tenant defense matter or a small-business formation, then edit it into your standard. Ask for a Spanish version and a plain-language version.
  • Screening scripts for your assistant. A short phone script that collects the names needed for a conflict check before any facts, tells callers the firm has not agreed to represent them, and avoids giving legal advice. That order matters, because the ABA notes that protections extend to prospective clients' information.
  • Non-engagement and declination letters. Templates that make clear no attorney-client relationship was formed and flag that deadlines may apply. You fill in names and dates in your own system.
  • Follow-up email sequences. Document request lists, appointment confirmations and "what to bring to your consultation" emails, written once and reused.

What does not go into ChatGPT: the caller's name, the other side's name, and the story they just told you. If you want help analyzing a new matter, describe it without identifiers (Florida and NYC both treat that as the line), or wait until you have the client's informed consent.

Engagement Letters: Draft the Template, Then Add an AI Paragraph

Engagement letters are the best use of ChatGPT in a solo practice because the work is drafting, and the drafts contain no client information until you fill them in. A workable approach:

  1. Build a master template per matter type. Scope of representation, what is excluded, fee structure, retainer and replenishment terms, file retention, termination and communication expectations. Ask ChatGPT to compare your current letter against a checklist of those items and point out gaps, using a copy with client details removed.
  2. Add a short AI-use paragraph. The NYC opinion says a lawyer "should consider disclosing to the client the intent to use Generative AI," particularly when explaining fees. A sentence or two saying the firm uses AI tools under business terms that exclude training on firm data, that a lawyer reviews all work product, and that the client can ask the firm not to use AI on their matter is a reasonable start.
  3. Do not treat that paragraph as consent for everything. None of the three opinions says a general clause is enough to put confidential information into an AI tool. NYC says consent must be "knowing" and the client must understand the consequences. If a matter calls for putting identifiable client facts into ChatGPT, have that conversation separately and document it.
  4. Get the fee language right. Florida's opinion says a lawyer must inform a client, "preferably in writing," before charging the actual cost of using generative AI, and that a lawyer "may not ethically prorate the periodic charges" of a subscription and should treat them as overhead. NYC says a lawyer "must not charge hourly fees for the time that would otherwise have been spent" without AI. For a solo on a $40-a-month workspace, the simple answer is overhead.

Conflict Checks: ChatGPT Is Not Your Conflicts Database

A conflict check is a search of names against your own records. ChatGPT Business does not hold your client list, and you should not paste one into it. The NYC opinion's requirement that AI systems implement "any ethical screens required under the Rules" is aimed at firms that build tools on top of their own data. For a solo, the safer reading is to keep conflicts in your practice-management software or your existing spreadsheet, and use ChatGPT to improve the process around it:

  • A written conflict-check procedure. When it runs (before the consultation, not after), which names you search (client, spouse, opposing party, opposing counsel, related entities, key witnesses), how you record the result, and who signs off.
  • A related-parties checklist by matter type. For a business dispute: owners, officers, parent and subsidiary companies, former names. For an estate: heirs, beneficiaries and fiduciaries. ChatGPT is good at listing who you might forget to search.
  • Spreadsheet help. If your conflicts list lives in Excel, ask for formulas that flag near-matches or alternate spellings, using made-up sample names to build them. See our guide to ChatGPT in Excel and Google Sheets.
  • Waiver and disclosure letter templates. Drafts for the situations you see repeatedly, which you then adapt and send from your own system.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a solo attorney use ChatGPT for client intake? ▼

Yes, to build the intake process: questionnaires, phone scripts and declination letters contain no client information. Be careful with AI chatbots that talk to prospective clients directly. Florida Bar Ethics Opinion 24-1 says prospective clients must be told they are communicating with an AI program, and NYC Bar Formal Opinion 2024-5 says a lawyer using a chatbot for intake must adequately supervise it.

Do I need client consent to use ChatGPT on a matter? ▼

Not if no confidential information goes in. The NYC Bar's Formal Opinion 2024-5 says consent is not needed if no confidential client information is shared, and Florida's Opinion 24-1 says the same when nothing confidential is disclosed to a third party. If you want to enter identifiable client facts, get informed consent first and check your own jurisdiction; California's 2026 guidance is stricter.

Is an AI paragraph in my engagement letter enough? ▼

It is a good disclosure, but none of the ABA, Florida or NYC guidance says a general clause is enough consent to put confidential information into an AI tool. NYC says advance consent must be knowing and the client must understand the consequences. Use the paragraph to explain how the firm uses AI, and have a separate conversation if a matter calls for entering client details.

Can ChatGPT run my conflict checks? ▼

No. A conflict check searches names against your own client records, and those should stay in your practice-management software or conflicts list. Use ChatGPT to write the conflict-check procedure, related-party checklists by matter type, spreadsheet formulas built on sample names, and waiver letter templates.
